Modes of convergence and Bayes' theorem

The first part compares the ways in which a sequence of random variables can converge, their roles in the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem, and why √n appears so often. The second part covers conditional probability, Bayes' theorem and how prior, likelihood and posterior work together in Bayesian inference.

1 The main modes of convergence

Let X₁, X₂, … and X be random variables on the same probability space (Ω, P).

  • In probability, Xₙ →ᴾ X: for every ε > 0, P(|Xₙ − X| > ε) → 0. For large n a big deviation is unlikely, but it can still happen at some n, and at different n for different outcomes ω.
  • Almost surely, Xₙ →ᵃ·ˢ· X: P({ω : Xₙ(ω) → X(ω)}) = 1. Each single sequence of outcomes converges, except on a set of probability 0: after some point (depending on ω) the deviations stop for good.
  • In mean square (L²): E[(Xₙ − X)²] → 0. The average squared error vanishes; it requires finite second moments and controls the size of the errors, not only their probability.
  • In distribution, Xₙ →ᵈ X: Fₙ(x) → F(x) at every point where F is continuous. Only the distributions get close: Xₙ and X need not even be defined on the same space.

How they are related

Almost sure ⇒ in probability ⇒ in distribution, and L² ⇒ in probability (by Chebyshev's inequality, P(|Xₙ − X| > ε) ≤ E[(Xₙ − X)²]/ε²). In distribution to a constant c ⇒ in probability to c. No other implication holds in general:

  • In probability and in L², but not almost surely. Independent Xₙ ~ Bernoulli(1/n): P(Xₙ = 1) = E[Xₙ²] = 1/n → 0, but Σ 1/n = ∞, so by the second Borel–Cantelli lemma Xₙ = 1 for infinitely many n with probability 1, and almost no sequence converges to 0.
  • Almost surely, but not in L². Independent Xₙ = n with probability 1/n² and 0 otherwise: Σ 1/n² < ∞, so by the first Borel–Cantelli lemma only finitely many Xₙ are non-zero and Xₙ → 0 almost surely, while E[Xₙ²] = n²·1/n² = 1 for every n.
  • In distribution, but not in probability. X ~ Bernoulli(½) and Xₙ = 1 − X for every n: Xₙ has exactly the distribution of X, but |Xₙ − X| = 1 always.

Simulation of the first counterexample

m sequences X₁, X₂, … with independent Xₙ ~ Bernoulli(1/n) are simulated up to n = 10⁹. Since P(no 1 among Xₙ₊₁, …, Xⱼ) = n/j, after a 1 at position n the next one is at ⌊n/U⌋ + 1 with U uniform on (0, 1), so only the positions of the ones need to be generated.

The probability of a 1, and E[Xₙ²], fall by a factor 10 in every block, so Xₙ → 0 in probability and in L². Yet in every block about 90% of the sequences still contain a 1, on average ln 10 ≈ 2.3 of them: the deviations never stop, and on almost every sequence Xₙ does not converge.

2 Their roles in the LLN, the CLT and other results

X₁, X₂, … are independent and identically distributed with mean μ and variance σ², and X̄ₙ = Sₙ/n.

  • Weak Law of Large Numbers: in probability. X̄ₙ →ᴾ μ. With σ² < ∞ it follows from Chebyshev, P(|X̄ₙ − μ| > ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²), as checked in HMWK 3. In statistics this is the definition of a consistent estimator: with enough data the estimate is close to the true value with high probability.
  • Mean square. E[(X̄ₙ − μ)²] = σ²/n → 0, so X̄ₙ → μ in L², which gives the weak law and also the rate. The mean squared error of an estimator, MSE = variance + bias², is the L² distance from the true value, the usual measure of its quality.
  • Strong Law of Large Numbers: almost surely. X̄ₙ →ᵃ·ˢ· μ, assuming only E|X| < ∞ (Kolmogorov). It is a statement about each single infinite sequence of observations: the running average of one attacker's trajectory in HMWK 3 converges with probability 1. It justifies the frequency interpretation of probability and Monte Carlo simulation, where only one long run is observed.
  • Central Limit Theorem: in distribution. √n·(X̄ₙ − μ)/σ →ᵈ N(0, 1). It does not say that X̄ₙ converges to something new, but describes the shape and the size of its error, and it cannot be strengthened: the standardized mean keeps fluctuating and does not converge in probability. It is the basis of approximate confidence intervals and tests.
  • Other results. The Glivenko–Cantelli theorem: the empirical distribution function converges to F almost surely and uniformly, the basis of the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test used in HMWK 3. Slutsky's theorem: if Xₙ →ᵈ X and Yₙ →ᴾ c, then Xₙ·Yₙ →ᵈ cX, which allows replacing σ by the sample standard deviation s. The delta method: √n·(g(X̄ₙ) − g(μ)) →ᵈ N(0, g′(μ)²σ²). Maximum likelihood estimators are consistent (in probability) and asymptotically normal (in distribution).
