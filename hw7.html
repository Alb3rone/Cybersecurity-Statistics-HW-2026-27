Contingency tables and the χ² test of independence

A bivariate dataset (X, F) is summarized in a contingency table, from which marginal and conditional frequencies are computed. Comparing the observed counts with those expected under independence gives the χ² statistic and its p-value, and tells whether the two variables are statistically independent.

1 Data and contingency table

The default dataset is the graduate admissions of the University of California, Berkeley, in fall 1973, for its six largest departments (Bickel, Hammel and O'Connell, Science 187, 1975): 4,526 applicants, each recorded with department (A–F), gender and outcome. The program builds the list of records and counts them; any other dataset can be pasted as lines "X, F".

2 Expected frequencies and χ² statistic

If X and F were independent, the joint relative frequency would be the product of the marginals, so the expected count in cell (i, j) is Eᵢⱼ = nᵢ.·n.ⱼ/n. The χ² statistic adds up the squared differences between observed and expected counts, each relative to the expected count: χ² = Σᵢ Σⱼ (nᵢⱼ − Eᵢⱼ)²/Eᵢⱼ. Under independence it follows approximately a χ² distribution with (r − 1)(c − 1) degrees of freedom, where r and c are the numbers of rows and columns, provided the expected counts are at least about 5. The p-value is the probability of a value at least as large: p = P(χ²_df ≥ χ²) = Q(df/2, χ²/2), computed with the regularized incomplete gamma function.

3 Independence in probability and in statistics

  • Events. Two events A and B are independent when P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B); if P(B) > 0 this is the same as P(A | B) = P(A): knowing that B happened does not change the probability of A.
  • Random variables. X and F are independent when every event about X is independent of every event about F. For discrete variables this means P(X = xᵢ, F = fⱼ) = P(X = xᵢ)·P(F = fⱼ) for every pair of values: the joint distribution is the product of the marginals, and the conditional distribution of F is the same for every value of X.
  • In a sample. The probabilities are unknown and are estimated by relative frequencies. Independence means that the rows of the conditional table of section 1 are all equal to the marginal row, or equivalently that the joint frequencies are the products of the marginal ones. In a finite sample this never holds exactly, so the question is whether the differences are larger than sampling variability would explain.
  • The test. The χ² test answers that question. The null hypothesis is independence; the p-value is the probability, under that hypothesis, of a discrepancy at least as large as the observed one. A small p-value (below 5%) leads to rejecting independence: the variables are statistically dependent, or associated. A large p-value does not prove independence, it only means that the data are compatible with it.

4 Interpretation: association is not causation

For gender × admission, χ² ≈ 92 with 1 degree of freedom and p ≈ 10⁻²¹: gender and admission are strongly dependent. Overall 44.5% of the men and 30.4% of the women were admitted. Department × admission and department × gender are dependent too: the departments admit very different shares of applicants, from 64% in A to 6% in F, and men and women applied to different departments. Repeating the test inside each department tells a different story.

  • The association disappears within departments. In five departments out of six the admission rates of men and women are close and the test does not reject independence. In department A the difference is significant, but in favour of women.
  • A confounding variable explains it. 93% of the women applied to the departments with low admission rates (C to F), while about half of the men applied to A and B, where the rates are high. The overall dependence between gender and admission comes from the department, which is related to both: this is Simpson's paradox.
  • Association versus causality. The χ² test only says that two variables are not independent in the data. It does not say which one acts on the other, nor whether a third variable drives both. The table alone would suggest discrimination against women, but the per-department analysis shows that the gap is explained by the choice of department. Establishing causality requires controlling for confounders or a randomized experiment, not a test of independence.
