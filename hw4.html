Penetration score: LLN and CLT

Each of m attackers tries to penetrate n systems, one after the other. The score obtained on each system is a random increment, and the sum of the increments is the attacker's penetration score. Simulating many attackers shows how the total, the average and the normalized total behave as the number of systems grows.

1 The model

Attacker j meets the systems in order and gets a score Xᵢ on system i. The increments X₁, X₂, … are independent and identically distributed, with mean μ and variance σ². After k systems the attacker's score is Sₖ = X₁ + … + Xₖ, so every attacker draws one trajectory S₁, S₂, …, Sₙ and the m attackers give m independent trajectories.

  • Bernoulli model. Xᵢ ∈ {0, 1}, with P(Xᵢ = 1) = p: system i is either penetrated (1) or not (0), and Sₙ counts the penetrated systems. μ = p, σ² = p(1 − p).
  • Rademacher model. Xᵢ ∈ {−1, +1} with probability ½ each: every system either adds or removes one point, the symmetric zero-mean case. μ = 0, σ² = 1.

Mean and variance of the three quantities

Since the increments are independent, means and variances add: E[Sₙ] = nμ and Var(Sₙ) = nσ². Dividing by a constant c divides the variance by c².

4 Law of Large Numbers: convergence in probability of Sₙ / n

Sₙ/n converges in probability to μ if, for every ε > 0, P(|Sₙ/n − μ| > ε) → 0 as n → ∞. The probability is estimated as the share of attackers whose average score is more than ε away from μ.

6 What it means for n systems and m attackers

System size n

  • The total score grows, and so does its uncertainty. An attacker facing more systems collects more points on average, nμ, but the spread between attackers also grows, like σ√n. In absolute terms two attackers become more and more different as the infrastructure grows.
  • The average score becomes predictable. The uncertainty grows more slowly than the total: relative to n it shrinks like 1/√n. This is the Law of Large Numbers: in a large infrastructure every attacker penetrates almost exactly a share p of the systems, and the individual luck of an attacker stops mattering. For a defender, p is the quantity that decides the expected damage, and n only scales it.
  • Fluctuations have a universal shape. The deviation of the total from nμ, measured in units of σ√n, follows a standard normal distribution for large n (section 5). So for any n, about 2.3% of the attackers exceed nμ + 2σ√n, whatever the details of the single-system score.
  • The symmetric case. In the Rademacher model there is no drift: no attacker is favoured, the total wanders around 0 and after n systems it is typically within ±2√n. A large total is not evidence of a skilled attacker, but the expected size of a random walk.

Number of attackers m

  • Each attacker is one independent sample. m does not change the distribution of a single attacker's score: it changes how much of that distribution we observe. With few attackers the histograms of section 3 are rough; with m in the thousands they match the theoretical shape, and the estimate of the mean improves like 1/√m.
  • More attackers, more extreme outcomes. A score above nμ + 2σ√n is rare for one attacker (2.3%) but almost certain to appear among 100 attackers. The defender has to plan for the best of the m attackers, not for the typical one, and that grows with m.
  • The aggregate over all attackers. Summing the scores of all m attackers gives m·n independent increments: the aggregate has mean mnμ and standard deviation σ√(mn), so the aggregate penetration rate is known with precision σ/√(mn). Doubling either the systems or the attackers has the same effect on it.

In short, n controls the behaviour of a single trajectory: the total diverges, the average converges to μ and the normalized total keeps a stable normal shape. m controls how many trajectories are observed: it makes the estimates reliable and the extreme attackers more likely.

Limits of the model

  • Independence between systems. Real systems often share software and configurations, so one vulnerability can open many of them at once. Positively correlated increments make the variance of Sₙ larger than nσ², and if a common cause affects all systems the average Sₙ/n no longer concentrates around μ.
  • Same probability on every system. Systems differ in how well they are protected, so p is not the same everywhere. With independent systems the average still converges, but to the mean of the different pᵢ, and the variance is smaller than np(1−p).
  • Non-adaptive attackers. The model assumes the attacker does not learn: a real attacker who penetrates a system gains information that raises the chance on the next ones, so the increments are neither independent nor identically distributed.
