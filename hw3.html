ElGamal on secp256k1 and the Caesar cipher

The same message is encrypted with a toy public-key scheme built on the secp256k1 curve of the previous homework and with the Caesar cipher. Both schemes are then compared and attacked: the Caesar cipher through the statistics of the language, the toy scheme through its small private-key space.

1 Message

2 Toy ElGamal on secp256k1

The curve is the real secp256k1, y² = x³ + 7 over a 256-bit prime p, with base point G of prime order n. The only toy element is the private key d, which is restricted to 1 ≤ d < 2^b so that the attack in section 6 can run in the browser.

The algorithm and the role of the keys

  1. Key generation (receiver). Choose the private key d at random and publish the public key Q = dG. d stays secret and is the only thing needed to decrypt; Q lets anyone encrypt for the receiver.
  2. Encoding. The message is split into blocks of 30 bytes. A block, read as an integer m, becomes the x-coordinate x = 256·m + j, where j is the first value in 0 … 255 for which x³ + 7 is a square mod p. This gives a point M on the curve.
  3. Encryption (sender). For each block pick a fresh random ephemeral key r in 1 … n − 1 and send the pair C₁ = rG, C₂ = M + rQ. The point rQ is a one-time mask that only the sender and the receiver can compute.
  4. Decryption (receiver). Compute dC₁ = d·rG = r·dG = rQ and remove the mask: M = C₂ − dC₁, then m = ⌊x / 256⌋.

3 Caesar cipher

Each letter is replaced by the letter k positions later in the alphabet, wrapping from Z to A: c = (p + k) mod 26. Decryption shifts back, p = (c − k) mod 26. Case is kept, spaces and punctuation are left unchanged. The same key k is used to encrypt and to decrypt.

5 Statistical cryptanalysis of the Caesar cipher

A Caesar key moves every letter by the same amount, so the ciphertext keeps the letter distribution of the language, only shifted. The attack builds the distribution of English from a corpus and then, for each of the 26 possible keys, decrypts the ciphertext and measures with the χ² statistic Σ (Oᵢ − Eᵢ)² / Eᵢ how far its letter counts Oᵢ are from the counts Eᵢ expected in English. The key with the smallest χ² is the answer.

5.2 Effectiveness and ciphertext length

Random passages of the test part are encrypted with a random key and attacked. Besides letter frequencies, two alternatives are compared. Exhaustive search tries all 26 keys and keeps the decryption with the most words found in the corpus vocabulary. Bigram scoring sums log P(next letter | letter), estimated on the corpus, and keeps the most probable decryption.

5.3 Limitations

  • Length. Below about 20 letters the sample is too small: the measured distribution is dominated by chance and the wrong key often wins.
  • Known language. The reference distribution must match the language of the message. An Italian text scored against English frequencies, or a list of names, codes or abbreviations, can mislead the attack.
  • Unusual texts. A message written to avoid common letters, or made mostly of numbers, has a distribution far from the language and defeats the frequency test, while the dictionary and bigram scores degrade more slowly.
  • Only for monoalphabetic ciphers. The attack works because one key maps every letter in the same way. It does not apply as is to ciphers that change the substitution along the text.

Since there are only 26 keys, exhaustive search alone already breaks the Caesar cipher: the hard part is not trying the keys but recognising the right plaintext automatically. Letter frequencies need nothing but a 26-number profile and are independent of word boundaries; bigrams and dictionary words use more of the structure of the language, so they need shorter texts but a larger model.
